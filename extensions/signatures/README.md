This extension says how a person signs what they decided, how an organisation signs exactly what it issued, and how a check of those signatures reports what it found. It defines two assertions: the person's signature, made by the reviewer or the affirmer over the outcome they gave or the record they affirmed, and the organisation's seal, made by the issuer over an export, an action-binding attachment or a record. Each is an object beside the records, the export or the attachment. Neither adds a field to a decision record, and neither stands for the other.

It is the second of two related profiles agreed in issue #29. The first, [anchoring](../anchoring/) (an independent timestamp and an independent checkpoint), defines the signed-bytes rule, the key states and the trust policy that this profile reuses by reference: there is one trust policy, anchoring 0.2.0's, and it holds the keys of every role. This profile relies on anchoring's checks for one thing only: an independent timestamp over a signature value, which a compromised key's revocation needs.

Version 0.2.0 changes 0.1.0 after review, and some documents 0.1.0 allowed are refused: identity verified while the key's state is not, a passkey signature made from an outside page, a document naming a member twice, a key hex ending in a newline, an attachment's copy of its seal that differs from the seal in a member. Each document states its version, and every signature's bytes include it, so a 0.1.0 signature does not verify as a 0.2.0 one, and a 0.2.0 verifier does not read a 0.1.0 document. The list, and what to do with 0.1.0 documents, is under "Changes from 0.1.0".

## Scope, and what this extension does not claim

- The extension is optional. Its MUST, SHALL and SHOULD sentences bind only an implementation that says it uses signatures 0.2.0.
- It does not change what a core field means or when it is required. Where this text and the Standard's text disagree, the Standard's text governs.
- It says nothing about what any law requires. Whether a signature or a seal has legal effect is outside this extension. A qualified seal certificate is one source a verifier may configure; configuring one does not make a report a legal conclusion.
- The records remain input, not evidence. A verified person's signature shows that the holder of a key signed these bytes, with this outcome. A verified seal shows that the holder of the organisation's key issued these exact bytes. Neither shows that a record is true, complete or lawful, that the person read what they signed, or who held the key: only the verifier's trust policy links a key to a person or an organisation.
- Using this extension makes no one "certified", "compliant" or "approved".

## Signed bytes (the shared rule, by reference)

Every signature made under this profile is made over bytes built by anchoring 0.2.0's rule ("Signed bytes (shared rule)"): `"DPS-SIG" 0x00`, then each field length-prefixed with 4 big-endian bytes; a text field is the UTF-8 encoding of the JSON string's value, with no normalisation, its prefix counting bytes and not characters; a digest is its 32 raw bytes; a time has exactly the form `YYYY-MM-DDTHH:MM:SSZ` and is a real UTC time with no leap second; an optional value that is absent is signed as the empty byte string, a length prefix of `00000000` and nothing after it, the field keeping its place; the first four fields are always `profile` (`dps-signatures`), `profile_version` (`0.2.0`), the assertion type and `key_ref`; the verifier builds the bytes itself, from the fields it checks, never from bytes supplied with the signature; and a value the rule cannot encode, or a time that cannot be read, fails the check that needs it and never stops the verifier. For a signed time (`signed_at`, `sealed_at`) the check that needs it is `key_state`: the time enters the signed bytes as the text written, so `signature` is checked over the time as written and can verify, while the key's state at a time that cannot be read (30 February, 23:59:60) cannot be read either, and that dimension fails; the comparison of such a time with an independent timestamp is not made. This profile is where the rule's absent-value sentence applies: a review stage states a view digest or not, so `shown_view_digest` is signed as empty when the review states none, and nothing can be mistaken for it, since a digest is always 32 bytes. Anchoring has no optional field.

This profile signs four assertion types under the rule:

| Assertion type | Fields, in order |
|---|---|
| `person_approval` | 1 `profile`, 2 `profile_version`, 3 `person_approval`, 4 `key_ref`, 5 `decision_id`, 6 `stage_id` (the outcome stage), 7 `outcome` (`approved`), 8 `shown_spec_digest` (digest), 9 `shown_view_digest` (digest, or empty when the review states none), 10 `approved_spec_digest` (digest), 11 `signed_at` (time) |
| `person_review` | 1 `profile`, 2 `profile_version`, 3 `person_review`, 4 `key_ref`, 5 `decision_id`, 6 `stage_id`, 7 `outcome` (`rejected` or `escalated`), 8 `shown_spec_digest` (digest), 9 `shown_view_digest` (digest, or empty), 10 `signed_at` (time) |
| `person_affirmation` | 1 `profile`, 2 `profile_version`, 3 `person_affirmation`, 4 `key_ref`, 5 `decision_id`, 6 `affirmed_digest` (digest), 7 `signed_at` (time) |
| `organisation_seal` | 1 `profile`, 2 `profile_version`, 3 `organisation_seal`, 4 `key_ref`, 5 `sealed.kind` (`export`, `attachment` or `record`), 6 `sealed.id`, 7 `sealed.digest` (digest), 8 `sealed_at` (time) |

An approval and a review are two types rather than one type with a field that is sometimes there: an approval has one more field, the approved digest, and the rule wants one fixed order per type with no field that is sometimes present. The outcome is a field of both all the same, so that it is signed as a word and not only through the type. The view digest is the one place where the source itself is optional (a review stage states it or not), so it is always in the order and signed as empty when the review states none, rather than doubling the types. The time a signer states is signed in every type, because the key states need the signer's own statement, as the checkpoint of anchoring signs its `checkpoint_time`.

## The signatures attachment

An attachment made under this extension:

1. MUST identify the profile and its version (`dps-signatures`, `0.2.0`).
2. MUST carry each person's signature and each organisation's seal as its own object, with its assertion type, the fields of its order, and its signature: the algorithm, the `key_ref` and the value.
3. MAY carry independent timestamps over signature values, in anchoring 0.2.0's shape, each naming the assertion whose value it covers as anchoring names it.
4. MUST NOT carry a key, a certificate or an identity binding. It carries key references only: a verifier takes every source of trust from its own trust policy (portable verification, items 6 and 7 of its export rules).

An attachment is not sealed or chained. The `attachment_digest` in a report fixes which bytes were checked. The attachment sits beside the documents it concerns; an action-binding attachment MAY in addition carry a copy of the organisation's seal of it as its `issuer_signature` (below).

## The person's signature

The person who gave an outcome, or who affirmed a record, signs what they decided. Three assertion types:

- **`person_approval` and `person_review`, for an outcome recorded under action binding.** The signed fields are the record (`decision_id`), the outcome stage (`stage_id`), the outcome (`approved`, `rejected` or `escalated`), the digest of what the review showed (`shown_spec_digest`, and `shown_view_digest` when the review states one) and, for an approval, the `approved_spec_digest`. The outcome is inside the signed bytes, and an approval is its own type, so a signed rejection cannot be presented as an approval.
- **`person_affirmation`, for a Core record that has no action binding.** The signed field is `affirmed_digest`: the SHA-256 of the record's covered bytes, in portable verification's sense. Those are the exact bytes the Standard sealed (§5.1(3)): the seal field absent, or every character of its value replaced by `0`, as the export states in `seal_field_handling`; and `affirmation_record` as it stands in those bytes. That digest is the record's `seal_hash`. The issuer therefore writes the affirmation event (`affirmation_record`: timestamp, actor identity, method) before the person signs, and the person signs the content together with the issuer's statement of their own act. `affirmation_record.method` MAY name the signature by a reference allocated before signing; it never carries the signature value, which does not exist until the bytes are fixed. The signature is an object beside the record, never a field in it.

  Why these bytes. A verifier holding a portable verification export recomputes them without rewriting anything: the export carries exactly these bytes and says whether they include the affirmation record (`affirmation_record_covered`, which the report carries through as `covers_affirmation_record`). The person's signature then covers exactly what the seal covers, so the two agree on one value, and a later change to the affirmation record, another actor or another time, breaks the person's signature as it breaks the seal. The alternative, the covered bytes with the three values of `affirmation_record` each replaced by characters `0` of the same length, lets the person sign before the issuer writes the affirmation event, but makes the verifier locate and rewrite an object inside exact bytes, and leaves the issuer's statement of the person's own act outside the person's signature; it was not chosen. What this proves: the holder of the key signed this exact sealed record, including, when the seal covers it, the issuer's affirmation statement. What it does not prove: that the affirmation time is right, since the person endorsed the issuer's statement, and only an independent timestamp shows when the signature existed; nor anything in the list below.

- **Suites.** Ed25519 (RFC 8032) over the signed bytes themselves; and WebAuthn with ES256 (a passkey, `WebAuthn-ES256`). An authenticator does not sign arbitrary bytes: it signs `authenticatorData || SHA-256(clientDataJSON)`. The challenge the client puts in `clientDataJSON` is therefore the SHA-256 of the signed bytes, in base64url with no padding, as a browser writes it. The assertion carries the signature (the DER ECDSA-Sig-Value the authenticator returns), the `authenticatorData` and the `clientDataJSON`, as returned, so that the verifier can check them.
- **Source of trust.** The verifier's trust policy (anchoring 0.2.0's) binds `key_ref` to a person, in role `person`, and names the source that binding rests on (`identity_source_ref`: a directory, an identity provider, an enrolment record). This is the identity dimension. It is reported apart from the signature check. A passkey's entry also names the relying party identifier and the origins it may be used from (`webauthn`).
- **What it proves.** The holder of that key signed these bytes, with this outcome, over this record or this attachment. With WebAuthn, the UV flag shows that the authenticator verified its user locally; it is reported as a fact of the authenticator, never as identity.
- **What it does not prove.** That the person read or understood what they signed; that the view matched the action; who held the key, since only the trust policy links a key to a person; that the person had authority, which stays a separate assertion of action binding; any legal effect.

A verifier checks a person's signature as follows, and reports each as its own dimension:

1. `signature`: `key_ref` is a key in the trust policy, in role `person`, of the algorithm the signature names; and the signature verifies over the signed bytes the verifier builds. For `WebAuthn-ES256`, also: the policy's entry names a relying party identifier and origins (without them the signature fails); the client data has type `webauthn.get`, a challenge equal to the SHA-256 of the signed bytes, and an origin the policy allows for the key; the `authenticatorData` carries the SHA-256 of the policy's relying party identifier and the UP flag; and the ES256 signature verifies over `authenticatorData || SHA-256(clientDataJSON)` with the key's public key, the 65-byte uncompressed point (`0x04`, x, y): a point written compressed, in 33 bytes, fails here, since the text says which form the entry gives. The UP and UV flags are carried through (`authenticator`), with the client data's `crossOrigin` and `topOrigin` when it states them, as facts. A client data that states anything but `crossOrigin: false` or no `crossOrigin` at all (a same-origin ceremony writes nothing else, whatever the value's type), or that states a `topOrigin`, fails the signature: WebAuthn Level 3 (§13.4.9) has the relying party validate that a `topOrigin` is expected, the verifier plays that part through its policy, and the policy's entry cannot yet say which outside pages are expected. A list of allowed outside pages may come in a later version, for framed signing.
2. `subject`: the signed fields against the document they name. For an approval or a review, the action-binding attachment of the record: `stage_id` is an outcome stage with that outcome; its review shows that specification digest and that view digest (or none); for an approval, the outcome's approved digest is the signed one. For an affirmation, the export entry of the record: `affirmed_digest` is the entry's seal, and the covered bytes, when available, hash to it. The report names what it checked against.
3. `key_state`: the key's state when it signed, as anchoring 0.2.0 defines it, with `signed_at` as the signer's stated time. A `signed_at` that is not a real UTC time fails here, not in `signature`, which is checked over the time as written; so does such a time in the policy's entry for the key.
4. `identity`: the binding of the key to a person, from the policy and the source it names there, compared with the actor the issuer names: the outcome's `actor_ref`, or `affirmation_record.actor_identity` read from covered bytes that match the seal. It is verified only when the signature is, only when the key's state when it signed is (`key_state`), only when the policy names a source for the key, and only when the holder the policy gives is the actor the issuer names. When the key's state is not verified (a compromised key with no timestamp, a signature stated before `not_before` or after the key was superseded, a time that cannot be read), the signature does not show that the holder signed, since the key may have been anyone's by then: identity is not checked, and reported so, as when the signature is not verified; an actor other than the holder is still reported as inconsistent. It never rests on the signature, on the issuer's statement or on an authenticator flag.

The assertion is verified only when `signature`, `subject` and `key_state` are. Identity is not part of it: a verified signature with an inconsistent identity is reported as such, as a verified signature whose key the policy binds to someone else.

## The organisation's seal

The issuer's key over exactly what it issued: the issuer signature both other extensions describe, given a suite and exact signed bytes.

- **Signed fields.** What is sealed (`sealed.kind`: `export`, `attachment` or `record`), its identifier (`sealed.id`: the `decision_id` of a record or of an action-binding attachment; the issuer's own reference for an export, which states no identifier) and its digest (`sealed.digest`): the SHA-256 of the export's or the attachment's bytes as issued, or the record's `seal_hash`. And the time the issuer states (`sealed_at`).
- **Where the signature sits inside what it signs.** An action-binding attachment MAY carry a copy of its seal as its `issuer_signature`. The sealed digest of an attachment is then taken over its bytes with every character of `issuer_signature.value_b64` replaced by `A`. This keeps the length, as portable verification does with `0` for the seal field: the issuer signs with a placeholder of the signature's length in place (88 characters for Ed25519 in padded base64), then copies the value in. The characters replaced are the value's, 88 for Ed25519, not the writing's: JSON lets a writer escape `/` as `\/`, and the base64 alphabet holds `/`, so a written value may have more characters than the value; whatever the writing, the value between its quotes is replaced by as many `A` as the value has characters, and an escaped writing then gives the same sealed digest as an unescaped one, the rest being written the same. Replacing the written characters instead (92 where four `/` are escaped) gives another digest, and is not the rule. `tests/vectors.json` holds such a writing (`escaped_copy`), with both digests. A writer SHOULD write `value_b64` with no escape, which the alphabet needs none of, so that a verifier that re-serialises the attachment reproduces its bytes. The copy is action binding's `issuer_signature` member: `algorithm` and `key_ref` as the seal states them, `signed_bytes_ref` the text `dps-signatures 0.2.0 organisation_seal`, which names this profile's signed bytes for a seal, `value_b64` the seal's value, and, in an action binding 0.3.0 attachment, `sealed_at` as the seal states it, written in place and so under the sealed digest as well; a 0.2.0 attachment's copy has the first four members only. The verifier does the reverse and compares; it also reports whether the copy agrees with the seal (`embedded_copy`): verified only when every member does and the copy has exactly the members its version names, four or five, so that a copy labelled otherwise, naming other signed bytes, stating another time, or carrying a member a copy does not have, does not agree. The comparison goes by the attachment's `profile_version`, since a 0.2.0 copy cannot carry `sealed_at`. An attachment of a version this check does not know is not compared (`embedded_copy` not checked, with the reason), the sealed digest still checked. It never reads the key, or the signed bytes, from that copy. A document this profile reads, at any depth, that names a member twice is not read (as I-JSON, RFC 7493 §2.3, has it): a reader that keeps the last member and one that keeps the first read two different documents, and only the issuer can write that. The attachment's `issuer_signature`, the attachment naming `issuer_signature` itself twice, or an outcome stage naming its approved digest twice split readers the same way: the seal verifies for both, and one of them reads a placeholder. The reference check reads every document with duplicate members rejected: the client data, the covered bytes, its fixtures, the schemas and the anchoring vectors it loads.
- **Suites.** Ed25519 (RFC 8032). The policy MAY pin an organisation's key by an X.509 certificate as well (`certificate_b64`); the check then reads that the certificate holds the key. Building a chain to a trusted list, and checking a certificate's revocation status, are out of scope: a verifier that needs them does them before it pins a certificate. A qualified seal certificate is one example.
- **Source of trust.** The verifier's trust policy maps `key_ref` to an organisation, in role `organisation`. Whether a seal has legal effect stays outside the extension.
- **What it proves.** The holder of the organisation's key issued these exact bytes.
- **What it does not prove.** That the claims are true, complete or lawful; that any person reviewed anything; when it was issued, since `sealed_at` is the issuer's statement and only an independent timestamp shows when the signature existed.

A verifier checks a seal as follows, and reports each as its own dimension: `signature` (the key in role `organisation`, Ed25519, the pinned certificate holding the key when there is one, the signature over the signed bytes the verifier builds), `subject` (the sealed digest against the document: the record's seal in the export, and its covered bytes when available; the attachment's bytes as issued with the copy replaced by `A`; the export's bytes as issued, the identifier carried as the issuer's reference) and `key_state` (with `sealed_at` as the stated time; a `sealed_at` that is not a real UTC time fails here, the signature being checked over the time as written). The seal is verified only when all three are.

## Independent timestamps over signature values

A compromised key needs an independent timestamp earlier than `revoked_at` over the signature value (anchoring, "The trust policy and key states"). The attachment MAY carry such timestamps in anchoring 0.2.0's shape: an RFC 3161 token whose `messageImprint` is the SHA-256 of the raw signature value, with a reference to the authority, checked exactly as anchoring says (`token`, with its guards on `genTime` and `accuracy`; `message_imprint`; `subject_bytes`; `message_digest`; `tsa_signature`, the token's algorithm fitting the pinned key; `signer_certificate`, an ESSCertIDv2 made with another hash than SHA-256 read as if there were none; `tsa_state`), against the authorities the trust policy pins. The subject's `of` names an assertion of this profile as anchoring 0.2.0 names it: its type, its `key_ref` and its identifiers (`decision_id`, and for an outcome its `stage_id`; or `sealed_kind` and `sealed_id`), with `profile` (`dps-signatures`) optional. `subject_bytes` is verified when an assertion in the attachment has that signature value and is the one named; anchoring's own reference check, which holds checkpoints only, reports such a subject as not checked, and this profile's check verifies it. The reference check supports the token algorithms anchoring's does: ECDSA P-256 and RSA PKCS#1 v1.5, each with SHA-256; a supported label that does not fit the pinned key fails, and another algorithm is not supported. The signer's stated time (`signed_at` or `sealed_at`) is compared with T and reported apart, as anchoring reports the issuer's time: a stated time later than T is inconsistent, and the timestamp still stands; a stated time that is not a real UTC time is not compared (`not_checked`), and the timestamp still stands too, since such a time fails the assertion's own `key_state`, not the timestamp.

## The trust policy and key states

- The trust policy is anchoring 0.2.0's (`dps-anchoring-trust-policy`, version `0.2.0`, schema [`../anchoring/schemas/trust-policy.schema.json`](../anchoring/schemas/trust-policy.schema.json)): the verifier's own configuration, never taken from an attachment, an export or a token. This profile defines no policy of its own. One document holds the time-stamping authorities, the keys of every role (`person`, `organisation`, `witness`, with `key_ref`, `holder`, `role`, `algorithm`, `public_key_hex`, `not_before`, `not_after`, `revoked_at` and `revocation_reason` as anchoring defines them) and the checkpoint sources. This profile reads the authorities and the keys; it does not read the checkpoint sources, which a verifier that uses no anchoring source lists as empty (`[]`).
- What this profile requires of a key, by role. Anchoring's key object admits three members it ignores, and this profile says when they are required: a person's key names the source its binding rests on (`identity_source_ref`); without one, the identity dimension fails, the signature still being checked. A passkey (`WebAuthn-ES256`) names its relying party identifier and the origins it may be used from (`webauthn`), and its public key is the 65-byte uncompressed point; without them, the signature fails. Where a signature is verified, `public_key_hex` is read strictly, lowercase hex pairs and nothing else: a value the schema's pattern lets through, such as a final newline (which `$` matches before in some engines), is not a key, and the signature fails with the reason, for a passkey as for an Ed25519 key. The refusal of a policy that lists one key twice keeps the lenient read, so that a copy written with a stray newline is still refused as the same key. An organisation's key may be pinned by a certificate (`certificate_b64`); the check then reads that the certificate holds the key, and a certificate that cannot be read fails the signature. The shared schema cannot see roles, so the check reports these, as it reports a time it cannot read. A member a role has no use for (a certificate on a person's key, an identity source on an organisation's key) is ignored.
- **One public key has one key reference, one holder and one role, and each reference names one entry** (anchoring, "The trust policy and key states"). A key in role `person` cannot make an organisation's seal, and the reverse; the issuer's key cannot be a witness. The check refuses a trust policy that lists the same public key under two references, two holders or two roles, or that gives one reference (a key's, an authority's or a source's) to two entries, whatever the entries say. The comparison is of the public key's bytes, decoded, whatever the entries' algorithm labels say and however the key is written: the same 32 bytes labelled `Ed25519`, `EdDSA` or `ed25519` are one key; a P-256 point written compressed (33 bytes) and uncompressed (65 bytes) is one key; an RSA key is one by its modulus and exponent, whichever DER structure carries it. The label plays no part in the refusal. It is read where a signature is verified: a label the check does not support is reported as not supported, and a signature whose label is not its entry's fails. Two different keys under one label are two keys, and are accepted. The refusal is reported once (`trust_policy`: `failed`, with the reason, which names the labels or the lengths when the entries of one key differ in them) and consistently: every dimension that relies on the policy is failed with the same reason ("... not checked: the trust policy is refused (...)"): for an assertion its signature, key state and identity, so the assertions all fail; for a timestamp its `tsa_signature` and `tsa_state`. The checks that do not rely on the policy still run: the signed fields are compared with the documents, and a token's `token`, `message_imprint`, `subject_bytes` and `message_digest` are checked. A policy that lists an extra, distinct key is not refused for it. Anchoring 0.2.0's check refuses the same policies, its reason naming the references, the holders or the roles; this check's reason adds the labels or the lengths when the entries of one key differ in them.
- A time in the policy that is not a real UTC time, or a pinned certificate that cannot be read, does not refuse the policy: it fails the check that relies on that entry (`tsa_state` or `tsa_signature` for an authority, `key_state` or `signature` for a key).
- The key states are anchoring's, by reference: a signature is checked against the key's state when it was made; that time is the signer's own statement (`signed_at`, `sealed_at`; `time_basis: stated_time`) unless a verified independent timestamp covers the signature value, in which case the earliest T is used (`independent_timestamp`); only the stated time can show that a signature was made after `not_before`; a signature outside the validity fails; a key that was only superseded passes on its stated time if that is before `revoked_at`, reported as the signer's statement; a compromised key counts only with an independent timestamp earlier than `revoked_at`, and with none the time cannot be established (`unavailable`).
- A signed time is a real UTC time with no leap second: the date exists, the hours run from 00 to 23, and the minutes and seconds from 00 to 59. The schemas fix its form only; a time the form admits that cannot be read (30 February, 23:59:60) fails `key_state`, for a signed time (`signed_at`, `sealed_at`) as for a time in the policy's entry for the key, and is never a reason for the verifier to stop. The signature itself is checked over the time as written, since the time enters the signed bytes as text, and it can verify; the assertion fails all the same, by its key state. A timestamp over the signature value stands, and the comparison with the stated time is not made.

## The verifier and its report

A verifier that uses this extension:

- MUST take every source of trust from its own trust policy: never from an attachment, an export, an action-binding attachment or a token.
- MUST build the signed bytes itself, from the fields it checks and with the profile, version and assertion type it expects.
- MUST report each person's signature, each organisation's seal and each timestamp as its own dimension: `verified`, `failed`, `not_checked`, `not_supported` or `unavailable`. Each names its `key_ref` (or `tsa_ref`) and the document it was checked against. It MUST NOT merge them, with each other or with another extension's dimensions, into one pass, fail or compliance result. The report schema has no place for one.
- MUST report identity as its own dimension, apart from the signature check, naming the source the policy gives, the holder and the actor the issuer names; and MUST NOT report it as verified on the issuer's statement, on the signature, or on an authenticator's UV flag, which it carries through as a fact.
- MUST give exactly one result for each person's signature, each seal and each timestamp in the attachment, each with its index, and state their counts, the digest of the attachment, the digest of each document it checked against (the export, each action-binding attachment by its `decision_id`), and whether the trust policy was accepted.
- MUST refuse a trust policy that gives one public key two references, holders or roles, or one reference to two entries, and report the refusal consistently, as above.
- MUST report a signer's stated time later than an independent timestamp as inconsistent, apart from the timestamp.
- MUST say, for each key state, what its time rests on.
- MUST give a result for every document valid against these schemas, and never stop on one. A value it cannot encode, or a time or a certificate it cannot read, is reported as failed, with the reason, in the check that needs it.
- MUST state the scope of its conclusion. That scope never includes whether the person read or understood what they signed, whether the view matched the action, who held the key, the person's authority, any legal effect, the truth of the record's content, whether any person reviewed what an organisation sealed, or when it was issued.

JSON Schema cannot compare two documents, so a reader holding both the report and the documents it covers checks the counts, the digests, and that each dimension reported as verified is verified; the test runner does.

## Relation to the other extensions, and what comes next

This extension reuses, by reference: anchoring 0.2.0's signed-bytes rule, key states, trust policy and timestamp assertion; portable verification's export, covered bytes and seal field handling (items 2 to 5 of its export rules); and action binding's outcome and review stages and its `issuer_signature` member. A record exported under portable verification, its action-binding attachment and its signatures attachment are checked side by side, each under its own extension, against one trust policy. Using signatures requires using anchoring's checks only for a compromised key's revocation, and portable verification only for a person's affirmation or a record's seal read from an export.

Items 2 to 5 of portable verification's export rules are the same in 0.1.0, 0.2.0 and 0.3.0, so an export labelled any of them is read the same by this extension, and a seal over any of them verifies; the fixture exports in `tests/vectors.json` were relabelled `0.2.0` in #53 and `0.3.0` with portable verification 0.3.0, so that `seal_export` and the digests after it moved with them each time. No rule of this extension changed with it. Action binding 0.3.0 made the attachment's `issuer_signature` this profile's seal, carried as its five-member copy, as portable verification 0.2.0 did for an entry's signature; the fixtures' attachments are 0.3.0 since then, so `seal_attachment` and the digests after it moved with them, and the copy check compares a copy by its attachment's version. No rule of this extension changed with that either.

Not in this version, and future work:

- Other suites for the person's signature and the organisation's seal: RSA, with RFC 9500's published test keys as the time-stamp tokens already use, and other WebAuthn algorithms.

## Dependencies

The reference check and the vector generator use the two packages the anchoring extension uses, at the same pins, in `tests/requirements.txt` with their own dependencies:

- `cryptography` 50.0.2, for Ed25519, ECDSA P-256 (the passkeys and a time-stamping authority), RSA (the second time-stamping authority's tokens) and X.509 certificates.
- `asn1crypto` 1.5.1, for the RFC 3161 tokens over signature values.

The scripts use these libraries for every cryptographic step and implement no primitive themselves, because reference code gets copied. They import nothing from the other extensions' runners: the small pieces of anchoring this check needs (the shared rule, the key states, the policy's refusal, the token check) are written again from the anchoring 0.2.0 text, with the same results and the same wording; the check reads anchoring's trust-policy schema and its vectors from their own folder. The runners of the other extensions do not use these packages, and `tools/requirements.txt` is unchanged.

The exit code is 0 when every case behaves as expected and 1 otherwise. Every document a case gives is valid against its schema, unless the case says otherwise, and a case in which the reference check raises an exception fails, whatever it expects.

Every key in the vectors is a test key only. This profile's own keys are derived from a published text: the seed is the SHA-256 of `dps-signatures 0.1.0 test key ` followed by the key's name (the text of the first version, kept in 0.2.0 so that the test keys do not change). An Ed25519 key's seed is its private key (RFC 8032). A passkey's ECDSA P-256 private scalar is the seed read as a big-endian integer, modulo n - 1, plus 1. The witness key and the time-stamping authorities are anchoring 0.2.0's test keys, so that one policy holds a key of every role and the two sets of vectors agree: `witness-a-1`, `tsa-example-1` and `tsa-example-2` under anchoring's seed text, and the RSA authority (`tsa-rsa-1`), whose key is the 2048-bit RSA test key RFC 9500 §2.1 publishes as `testRSA2048`, reconstructed in `generate_vectors.py` from the parameters the RFC prints, as anchoring's generator does, and checked against anchoring's vectors; the authorities' certificates are read from those vectors. Never derive a real key this way, and never use a published key for anything real. The signatures are deterministic, Ed25519 by definition, ECDSA with the nonces of RFC 6979 and RSA PKCS#1 v1.5 by construction, so `vectors.json` is byte-reproducible, and the WebAuthn vectors, which the text treats as verification-only, are reproduced here too. Each assertion gives its exact signed bytes in hex (`signed_bytes_hex`); each passkey assertion gives its challenge and the bytes the authenticator signed; each token gives the bytes its imprint covers. Generating needs OpenSSL 3.2 or later, which the pinned `cryptography` includes; checking does not. The records and the export are those of the anchoring and portable verification cases, the trust policy is anchoring's, and the specification is action binding's.

## Changes from 0.1.0

For anyone who implemented the 0.1.0 text (#34). The rule agreed on #28 asks whether a change makes documents valid under the published text invalid; five changes since #34 did, so the version moves.

**What 0.1.0 allowed and 0.2.0 refuses:**

- A report that calls identity verified while the key's state when it signed is not verified (#36). Identity is now not checked there, an actor other than the holder still inconsistent, and the report schema admits only `failed` or `not_checked` for identity where the key state is not verified.
- A passkey signature made from an outside page: a client data stating a `crossOrigin` other than `false` or absent, or any `topOrigin` (#37, #39). It now fails the signature, both facts carried, and a report that calls it verified is refused.
- A document this profile reads that names a member twice, at any depth (#37, #39, #41). It is not read.
- A key hex with a final newline, which some schema engines let through, where a signature is verified (#37). It is not a key, and the signature fails; the policy's duplicate check keeps the lenient read.
- An attachment whose copy of its seal (`issuer_signature`) differs from the seal in a member (#36). The #34 text never named the `signed_bytes_ref` text and treated the `Ed25519` and `EdDSA` labels as one key, so a copy labelled `EdDSA`, or naming another `signed_bytes_ref`, verified under it. The copy now agrees only when every member does: algorithm, key reference, `signed_bytes_ref` (the text `dps-signatures 0.2.0 organisation_seal`) and value.

**Other changes, which refuse nothing a correct 0.1.0 document stated:** a passkey's public key is the 65-byte uncompressed point as written, a compressed point failing the signature (#36); the replacement rule is over the value's characters, whatever the writing (#36); every read the reference check makes is strict (#41).

**A 0.2.0 verifier and a 0.1.0 document.** The schemas pin `profile_version` to `0.2.0`, so a 0.1.0 attachment, assertion or report is not valid input to this version's check, and the check does not read it. Even read, a 0.1.0 signature would not verify: its signed bytes name `0.1.0` in their second field. Nothing re-signs a document: a 0.1.0 document stays a 0.1.0 document, checked by the 0.1.0 text and check as they stood at `828b80e`, the merge of #41, under which the points above are already refused. The test keys are unchanged, since their seed text keeps the first version's wording.
